Tug-E-Nuff Whip It Flirt Pole Review 2026: Is It a Good Fit for a Border Collie's Chase Drive? Bungee, Lures and Impulse-Control Games

Compare the bungee and lure options, then choose a chase game your dog can finish calmly.

Home›Blog›Whip It flirt pole review

The Tug-E-Nuff Whip It Flirt Pole is a good fit for a Border Collie that enjoys chasing a moving toy, will trade it after a catch and can walk away when play ends. The long lure gives you room to move the target without throwing it. That same reach can make the set awkward in a small room, and a dog that locks onto the toy after every catch may have a better time learning the game with a handheld tug first.

How much room does the Whip It actually need?

The lure assembly is longer than the pole. Tug-E-Nuff’s size and assembly notes list a 79 cm pole and an approximately 125 cm lure assembly for either version. Add the listed lengths and you get about 204 cm, or just over two metres, end to end before accounting for flex, stretch, your arm position or the dog’s path. Use that arithmetic to picture the kit, then walk the route with the dog out of the area to see what the game actually needs.

Picture the whole route from the dog’s point of view. If the lure passes a table leg, your dog may try to cut inside it. If the lure ends up at a fence, the dog needs room to slow down after the catch. A spacious straight pass with room on both sides is a better starting exercise than swinging the lure around your feet. Check the ground from one end to the other, including where you stand.

Try tracing the path without moving the pole at full speed. Stand where you would start, lower the lure to the ground and walk through the pass. At the planned catch point, check that you can stop the pole and reach your reward without stepping over the line. If that feels awkward in a dry run, move the starting point or choose a handheld game for that space. A driveway with a clear straight line may still be a poor choice if you cannot keep it closed to vehicles and passersby.

What do the bungee and clip change?

The pole screws together and the lure clips onto it with a carabiner. Tug-E-Nuff describes the cord as reinforced bungee and recommends its own lures for the attachment. The give in the line is a reason to teach yourself to ease off as your dog catches the lure. Bring the pole to a stop, let the dog hold the soft target and leave enough slack to offer a calm trade. Treat the bungee as a feature of how the toy handles. The manufacturer’s description does not establish an injury reduction for this set.

Before play, check the joint, fasten the clip as the maker shows and inspect the fabric, attachment ring and line. End the game if you see damage or the dog grabs the line instead of the fabric. The maker specifies supervised, interactive use. Put the toy away between games, where your dog can go back to the rest of the household routine without standing guard over it.

The detachable lure is useful if you’d like to try a different version without replacing the whole kit. There’s also a trade-off: if you already own a favorite tug that makes trading easy, the maker advises against clipping arbitrary toys onto this pole. Practice separately with that tug while you work out whether you want the added reach. If your dog prefers bringing a thrown toy back to you, the Aero-Tug disc review covers a different play pattern.

Which lure should you choose?

I’d choose the no-squeak version for a first session with a sound-sensitive dog or one that already follows movement eagerly. Consider the squeaky version if familiar squeaky toys reliably draw your dog into play and the dog can still trade them. Start with the smallest amount of movement that gets interest, whichever lure you choose.

How can chase become an impulse-control game?

Make a short pause open the game and a voluntary drop earn a reward. The American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior recommends reward-based training. Restarting a game your dog wants to play is one way to apply that idea. You can also reward a drop with food if you have already practiced that trade with a familiar toy.

  1. Still lure, then go. Lay the lure still and start with a pause your dog can manage. Say your release cue before moving the lure. If your dog starts early, make the setup easier by asking for a shorter pause and working with a quieter toy if needed. Use a low pass across ground with grip.
  2. Catch, trade, restart. Let your dog catch the fabric. Stop the motion, give slack and cue the trade you have rehearsed with a handheld toy. Once your dog has let go and the area is clear, another release can start the next chase. If the trade falls apart, finish the pole game and rehearse it later in an easier setup.
  3. Catch, trade, finish. Use the same catch and trade, then give a consistent end cue and put the pole away. Offer an ordinary next activity your dog already knows, such as a sniff around the garden. The calm finish guide gives another example of planning what happens after an exciting event ends.

Change one part of the exercise at a time. If the wait went well with the lure still, repeat that easy start while you see whether your dog can trade afterward. If you lengthen the wait, keep the chase easy. The World Small Animal Veterinary Association’s play guide describes how games such as tug and fetch can give dogs a chance to leave a toy before play resumes. The same structure is the reason to include a clear restart here.

What tells you to end the round early?

Use the first missed easy trade, repeated line grabs or inability to turn away as a reason to end the round and simplify next time. Note which part became difficult. A dog that waits comfortably for the release but then keeps staring at the cupboard has given you different information from a dog that loses footing on a turn. For the former, try a quieter activity and a predictable finish. For the latter, abandon that route and look for a better surface and more space before the next session.

Keep the pole separate from situations involving other animals or traffic. Completing a wait beside a stationary lure tells you about that particular setup. If the concern is movement around children or cats, start with the household management steps in the guide to herding cats and children. For planning where chase fits alongside walks and quiet days, use the exercise and rest guide.

Write down what you want to repeat

A quick play record can be as simple as “paused for go, caught the fabric, traded on cue, sniffed after finish.” Add the surface and lure if you are comparing sessions. Suppose the first session with a silent lure ends comfortably and the next session with a squeaky lure ends with repeated staring at the toy. Repeat the silent setup before adding other changes. If both setups end the same way, go back to a stationary toy and practice the handover. The useful comparison is between your own dog’s responses under similar conditions.

Who should buy the Whip It?

Consider it if your dog clearly prefers chasing a moving target, enjoys catching it and can give it up, and you have room for a gentle pass. The replaceable lure and choice of sound are the strongest reasons to choose this set. If the same dog is happy playing a calm tug game in the space you already have, try the release and finish exercises with that toy before spending money on the pole.

Before you order

Check the play lane first. Try a calm toy trade next. Pick the lure based on the response you already see around sound. If you can describe how the first round will end, you have a better reason to buy than a general hope that the toy will wear your dog out.

Frequently asked questions

Should I choose the squeaky Whip It for a Border Collie?

Choose based on how your dog already responds to sound. I’d start with the no-squeak faux fur if movement alone gets a response or a squeaker makes trades harder. Consider squeaky if your dog likes familiar squeaky toys and can still give one up comfortably.

Can I clip my dog’s favorite tug onto this pole?

Tug-E-Nuff recommends the compatible Whip It lures and says other toy handles may fray at the attachment. Keep the favorite tug as a separate reward and use it to teach your start and trade cues before bringing out the pole.

How long should the first chase session be?

Plan an easy catch and a clean trade as your first goal, with an ordinary activity ready for afterward. Watch the quality of the trade and whether your dog moves on when the lure disappears. Those observations give you a starting point for the next session.

Does the bungee mean I can play tug with the pole?

In this routine the catch ends the moving part of the game. Give slack and trade the lure once the dog has it. If your dog likes sustained pulling games, use a separate handheld tug so you can practice the same release with a simpler setup.

Can a flirt pole fix chasing cats or bikes?

Use it for a defined game in a clear, enclosed space. Keep real-world chase triggers separate and manage access to them. If your dog can move away from a caught lure, record that as progress in the game and work on the other situation independently.
