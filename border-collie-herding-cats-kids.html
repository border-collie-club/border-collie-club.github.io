How to Stop a Border Collie Herding Cats and Kids (Without Killing the Instinct)

A practical household plan for stopping pursuit, rewarding disengagement, and making room for rest.

Home›Blog›Herding cats and kids

Draft prepared: 2026-10-14 · Border Collie Club

Learning how to stop a Border Collie herding cats and kids can feel like taking a second job: you sit down, someone moves, and you are managing another chase. You can make the household easier without turning every evening into obedience practice. Start with separation that lets everyone breathe, then teach your dog a small, repeatable alternative to following movement.

Quick answer: Prevent access to the chase, reward your dog for looking away before following starts, and practice returning to you or a resting station away from the action. Keep cats free to retreat and children out of training setups. You are teaching household boundaries, not promising to erase an instinct.

Which starting step fits your household?

This planning tool selects a conservative first action from your observations. It is not a behavior assessment or permission to remove barriers.

Choose your observations, then select “Show my starting plan.”

What should you observe before calling it herding?

Record what your dog actually does: looks, follows, blocks, circles, chases, or makes contact. Calling all of it “herding” does not tell you which situation to change first. Work from observable behavior rather than guessing a motive.

Write down the moment before the chase

Use a simple note: “Cat crossed the kitchen doorway; dog stopped chewing, stared, then followed.” For children, record the movement and setting, such as a hallway dash or a loud game after school. Include where the adult was and which barrier was available. This turns “he does it constantly” into a situation you can reorganize.

A 2026 Border Collie owner discussion describes a dog chasing a household cat that then spends much of its time hiding. That is one owner's report, not evidence of how common the problem is or proof of a successful treatment. It highlights a useful question: can the cat still use the home, or is the dog's access deciding everything?

Choose an outcome you can see

Replace “be less obsessive” with “stay on this side of the barrier while the cat uses the hallway.” Replace “respect the kids” with “return to the adult when an adult walks across the room.” If you want broader background on patterns of repetitive fixation in Border Collies, keep that reading separate from your training log; breed-level information cannot explain your individual dog's behavior.

How do you stop the next chase today?

Arrange the space before the busy moment starts. Use a secure dog-free area for the cat and a separate resting area for the dog during children's active play. If you cannot actively supervise, keep them apart rather than testing a cue.

Make the floor plan do some work

Draw the room layout and mark the places where paths cross. A mat beside the cat's doorway is a poor starting station: the dog can still monitor the route. Put the station farther from household traffic. Check whether a gate really contains your particular dog; use a closed door when it does not. Do not rely on furniture or your voice as the boundary.

The Association of Pet Behaviour Counsellors' introduction guidance recommends short sessions, rewarding looking away, and setting distance according to the animal that is more worried or aroused. It also stresses that lying down is not automatically relaxation. A dog flat on the floor but intensely watching the cat has not met your goal.

Give yourself permission to use management

On an exhausting day, close the door, meet each animal's ordinary needs separately, and skip the training experiment. Separation should come with a comfortable space and attention, rather than being a dramatic consequence. Your plan has to work while you make dinner, answer the door, or help with homework. A system that requires uninterrupted vigilance will be difficult to maintain.

How can you teach “look away and come back”?

First teach the return in a quiet place without the cat or children present. Reward a turn toward you, then practice the same skill around very small distractions. Do not begin with the most exciting event in your home.

Build an easy return before adding movement

Choose a cue such as “this way.” Say it once while your dog is already able to respond, move a little away, and reward beside you. Release your dog to a neutral activity, then repeat only while the exercise stays easy. The goal is a clear route back to the adult, not a long performance of sit, down, stay, and leave it.

For your practice notes, distinguish “turned without a cue” from “turned after a cue.” Both can be useful, but they describe different achievements. Reward voluntary check-ins as well as requested ones. Avoid presenting the cue as a challenge that the dog must pass while staring at an inaccessible cat.

Change one difficulty at a time

Use an adult helper for movement practice, with the dog separated by a secure barrier. Start with something ordinary, such as the helper shifting position. If the dog remains able to turn back readily, later try a slow step. Keep the barrier, speed, and distance consistent while you change that single movement. These are suggested practice examples, not a tested protocol with a guaranteed success rate.

If staring takes over, stop the movement and end the setup calmly. Next time use a less distracting position or more distance. Do not repeatedly call the dog while the helper keeps walking. During a Border Collie's teenage phase, use the same practical rule: select the version of the skill your dog can do today, rather than comparing it with last month's best performance.

What changes when the target is a cat?

The cat needs control over access and retreat; it should not become your training prop. Start with separation and skills away from the cat. Any later observation session must end if either animal cannot remain comfortable.

Audit the cat's ordinary routes

Walk through the cat's day on paper: resting place, food, water, litter area, and favored room. Does each route require passing the dog? If so, revise the arrangement before working on closer contact. An elevated perch may be useful, but do not treat it as permission for the dog to patrol underneath. Include a dog-free room the cat can use without negotiation.

Do not carry the cat toward the dog, trap it in your arms, or coax it through a doorway to see whether training “worked.” Your target outcome is an uneventful household routine. The animals do not need to cuddle, share a bed, or pose for a photo.

Notice small changes without granting new freedom

Record whether the dog can disengage and whether the cat voluntarily uses its usual spaces. A calm glimpse through a barrier is a useful observation, but not a reason to open it. Keep your notes descriptive: “Cat crossed upstairs landing; dog remained with adult downstairs.” That gives you a clearer record than “they are friends now.”

What changes when the target is a child?

The adult owns the training and supervision. Separate the dog before running games, loud play, or any situation you cannot closely manage. Children should not be responsible for interrupting, correcting, or proving the dog's reliability.

Make a family routine, not a child-led drill

Dogs Trust's guidance for dogs and children centers close attention, stepping in, and separation when needed. It also asks families to give dogs space while eating or resting and let them choose whether to approach. Use that principle to keep both playtime and quiet time predictable.

For a busy afternoon, decide who moves the dog to its resting area before the children begin playing. Prepare the space first so nobody is juggling treats, a gate, and homework at the last minute. If only one adult is available, choose separation over a movement-training session. Crawling babies and unpredictable toddler movement belong outside the practice setup.

Practice with adults instead

An adult helper can pause immediately and repeat a small movement consistently. A child should be free to play without becoming the distraction in a training experiment. Start behind a barrier and keep the child's routine separate. If you want the dog included during a calm family activity later, discuss the arrangement with a qualified reward-based professional who can observe your dog and home.

How do you preserve enjoyable activities without endless exercise?

Keep enjoyable dog activities separate from the people and animals being followed. Choose a manageable routine with clear starts, finishes, and quiet time afterward. You do not have to fill every available minute with stimulation.

Give activities a beginning and an end

Try a simple sequence in a separate space: an easy attention cue, a brief toy game your dog already enjoys, then put the toy away and move to a quiet activity. Alternatively, hide a few pieces of the dog's usual food for a sniffing search. Treat these as menu choices, not an obligation to add everything to every day.

Keep a practical question in mind: can I end this activity without launching another demanding session? If the answer is no, simplify the setup. There is no promise here that a particular game will satisfy or remove herding behavior. It is an organized alternative that does not involve controlling cats or children.

Practice an actual resting station

In this 2020 tutorial, Emily Larlham of Kikopup demonstrates calm settling, distinguishes relaxing from performing a down, and discusses reward expectations; watch it to plan quiet practice away from household movement.

Keep the mat somewhere that allows disengagement, not beside the target. Practice when nothing exciting is happening. If you need to leave the house, plan that separately using our guide to building manageable alone-time routines; a household barrier arrangement does not establish that your dog is comfortable alone.

A step-by-step plan for a less exhausting week

Use this as a flexible planning checklist, not a seven-day deadline for changing behavior. Repeat an easy stage as needed. Keeping the household workable is an outcome in its own right.

  1. Choose the hardest recurring moment. Pick the kitchen doorway or after-school playtime. Do not try to solve every setting at once.
  2. Rearrange access. Set up the cat's routes or the dog's separate resting area before that moment occurs. Check the boundary before relying on it.
  3. Agree on one adult action. Write “dog rests in the back room before running games” where the family can see it. Replace vague instructions such as “watch the dog.”
  4. Practice the return away from the target. Prepare a reward, use your chosen cue once, and reward beside you. Finish while the exercise remains straightforward.
  5. Plan an optional adult-helper session. Only do it when you can give full attention. Keep physical separation and begin with minimal movement.
  6. Log the setup, not just the result. Record the location, barrier, movement, and whether the dog turned freely. Note what you will make easier next time.
  7. Schedule your own break. A day of ordinary care and reliable separation can be enough. Ask another adult to cover the routine when possible.

A log entry you can reuse

Copy these prompts: “Situation: __. Boundary: __. First behavior noticed: __. Adult action: __. Dog's response: __. Cat or child's ability to continue their routine: __. Next adjustment: __.” Compare similar settings rather than adding a score that combines everything. Keep days with no practice in the log too; they help show whether the management arrangement is usable.

Common mistakes and when to get individual help

Do not measure success by how long you can delay a chase while repeating cues. Make the setup easier and reward the alternative earlier. If chasing or contact continues, keep physical separation and arrange individual reward-based training support.

  • Rewarding only after the chase. Set up opportunities to reward turning away before pursuit. If a chase occurs, separate first and review how access happened.
  • Using a still cat as proof. Evaluate your dog's ability to disengage rather than treating a motionless target as a completed training test.
  • Asking the cat to teach the dog. Do not build the plan around confrontations between the animals.
  • Giving children the leash or correction job. Keep responsibility with an adult and separate before active play.
  • Making everything harder together. Do not reduce distance, increase movement, and remove a barrier in the same experiment.
  • Adding sessions when you are worn out. Use the prepared management routine and return to practice when you can pay attention.

Seek help if you cannot create dependable separation, your dog persistently monitors barriers, or the cat or children cannot use their normal spaces comfortably. Bring your floor plan and observation log. Ask the professional to explain the intended alternative behavior, how sessions will stay manageable, and what would make them stop a setup. Do not request a guarantee that instinct will disappear.

Frequently asked questions

Will teaching my dog to leave the cat alone remove its instinct?

This plan teaches household boundaries and alternative actions. It does not claim to erase an instinct. Keep enjoyable dog activities separate from cats and children.

Should I let my cat correct my Border Collie?

Do not make confrontations part of the training plan. Give the cat dog-free routes and space, manage the dog’s access, and practice attention skills away from the cat.

Can my children help teach the dog not to herd?

Adults should manage training and supervision. Use an adult helper for controlled movement practice and keep children out of the training setup.

How long will this take?

There is no reliable deadline for your individual household. Track disengagement and workable routines in comparable settings. Do not remove barriers because a set number of days has passed.

What if my dog ignores leave it when someone moves?

Stop testing the cue in that setup. Maintain separation and practice an easier return away from the target. For persistent chasing or contact, seek individual reward-based support.

Can I skip training when I am exhausted?

Yes. Use a prepared separation routine, meet each animal’s ordinary needs, and return to practice when you can give it your attention. Management remains part of the plan.

A calmer household is a useful goal

To stop a Border Collie herding cats and kids, start by making unwanted pursuit unavailable and giving your dog an easy alternative away from the action. Keep adult responsibility clear, let the cat retreat, and use barriers when you need a break. You can value your dog's enthusiasm while setting firm household boundaries; you do not have to spend every evening proving that through another training session.
