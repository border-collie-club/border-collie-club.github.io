Why Your Border Collie Gets Excited When Work Calls End—and How to Teach a Calm Finish

A simple transition routine, separate cue rehearsals, and a quick log for the moment your meeting finishes.

Home›Blog›Calm call endings

By Callum Reid · Border Collie Club

When your Border Collie gets excited when work calls end, the difficult moment may be the transition itself: you say goodbye, move your hand toward the laptop, and suddenly a toy lands in your lap. You need a workable way to finish a note or move to your next task, without turning every meeting into a training project. Start by separating the signals your dog notices from the moment you actually become available.

Quick answer: Observe which part of the ending comes first, practice that signal gently outside real meetings, and reward an easy alternative such as resting nearby. Add a separate invitation for interaction that you can reliably honor. The outline below is a practical adaptation of reward-based training principles, not a tested Border Collie protocol or a promise of results.

Why does the end of a call seem to switch your dog on?

A familiar ending may have become a predictor of attention, movement, or play. That is a useful working hypothesis to investigate, not a conclusion about your dog's emotions. The important question is what changes immediately before your dog gets up.

Look for the earliest observable signal

In a 2023 Border Collie owner discussion, one person described their dog pestering them as a phone call wound down, even before goodbye, and recognizing the sound of an Xbox switching off. It is an anecdote, not a measure of how common this behavior is. It does suggest looking beyond a single word.

Write a short sequence from an ordinary call: your voice changes, you lean back, you remove a headset, you touch the lid, and you stand up. Mark where the dog first notices. Do not deliberately provoke a reaction to complete the list. If you cannot observe while working, ask another adult to note the sequence without interacting with the dog.

Keep interpretation modest

A dog bringing a toy does not establish that it understands your calendar, feels jealous, or needs more exercise. Record the action you can see. “Stood up when my hand touched the lid” is more useful for designing a small practice trial than “knows I am done with work.”

What does a calm finish actually look like?

Choose a small, observable outcome that leaves your dog comfortable and gives you room to finish your task. Your dog might briefly look up and then return to resting. The goal is not motionless obedience throughout an unpredictable workday.

Teach the resting skill away from calls

Dogs Trust's settle guidance recommends starting somewhere quiet, rewarding relaxed behavior, and adding distractions gradually. It also describes making a distraction easier when the dog cannot settle. Use that as your foundation rather than beginning with a busy meeting ending.

Choose a comfortable resting spot that your dog already likes. Practice when there is no goodbye, no meeting chime, and no need for you to answer a message. Distinguish being relaxed from holding a down while intensely watching your hands. You can reward an easy shift of attention away from you without demanding a long stay.

Define your own workable transition

Write down what you need to do after a meeting: save a file, type a note, or stand up and get water. Choose one of those tasks for practice. A successful transition should be something you can repeat in your actual routine, rather than an elaborate sequence you will abandon when work gets busy.

How do you separate goodbye, the meeting sound, and the laptop lid?

Practice them individually before combining them. Keep the rest of the setup as ordinary and quiet as possible, and stop if the dog becomes increasingly focused or restless. These are suggested rehearsal designs to review with a reward-based trainer, not established treatment steps.

Try the spoken ending by itself

Sit with your laptop idle and your dog already comfortable. Use an easy version of your signoff, then notice what happens before moving. If your dog stays relaxed or briefly checks and disengages, quietly reward that choice. Keep your hands away from the toy basket so the rehearsal does not accidentally include a second, stronger invitation.

If even a quiet fragment produces persistent staring or following, pause the experiment. Return to resting practice away from your work setup and discuss an easier starting point with a trainer. Repeating a full enthusiastic goodbye until the dog stops reacting is not the exercise proposed here.

Keep sound practice controllable

If your meeting software lets you preview the relevant sound at a low level, use that rather than starting and ending actual meetings repeatedly. First confirm that your device volume is under your control. Do not replay confidential calls, involve coworkers, or assume every version of Teams uses the same ending sound. If you cannot isolate the sound, leave that row of the plan unused.

Reduce the equipment movement

Start with reaching toward the lid, or a small lid movement, instead of shutting it and immediately standing up. Choose equipment and a moment when this will not interrupt your work. Never position your dog on the desk to make the trial more obvious. Record the version used so “lid practice” means the same thing when you compare sessions.

How can you make your availability clearer?

Choose a separate invitation for a real interaction, and say it when you are actually ready. A word has no automatic calming meaning: what follows it matters. Keep this invitation distinct from both your meeting goodbye and any release cue your dog already knows.

Give the invitation a specific consequence

For example, “your turn” could precede a quiet greeting or a familiar activity you can provide. Introduce it outside the work context, immediately before that interaction. Do not use it as another request to wait, and do not announce it while you are still typing. If the phrase already predicts an excited game, pick something else with your trainer.

Trainer Ken Ramirez's discussion of end-of-session signals explains that their effects depend on how they are used and what follows them. That is relevant here as a caution: calling a word “all done” does not make it relaxing. Our availability invitation is an editorial application of that distinction, not a protocol Ramirez tested for video calls.

Keep the promise small enough to honor

A calm greeting is easier to provide between tasks than a long play session. Continue to schedule ordinary walks, play, and attention independently of particular meeting noises. Your dog should not have to monitor every signoff to get its next opportunity for interaction.

If your main challenge is what happens when you leave the room or house, keep that separate. Our guide to building manageable alone-time routines addresses a different situation; practicing a quiet call ending does not show that your dog is comfortable alone.

A low-effort plan for your next workday

Prepare the routine before the first call, then use quiet moments for optional practice. You do not need to rehearse every signoff. On a busy day, preventing another difficult transition can be the whole plan.

  1. Choose one ending signal. Start with the one you can make easiest to control. Leave the other components out of that practice.
  2. Prepare the resting spot and rewards. Put them where you can use them without rummaging or reaching over the dog. Keep children and other pets out of the rehearsal.
  3. Write your immediate next task. Decide whether you are saving a file or offering interaction. Do not improvise the expectation after the dog has reacted.
  4. Run a brief, easy rehearsal outside a meeting. Notice the first response and reward an appropriate alternative before the setup becomes demanding.
  5. Log what actually happened. Select the signal, practice or real context, and response in the tool above. Describe changes in the setup in your own notes.
  6. Finish with an achievable routine. Provide the interaction you promised when available, or use a familiar resting arrangement. Skip further rehearsals if you cannot give them attention.

Plan for the unavoidable busy call

Use an arrangement your dog already accepts: a comfortable resting place, another adult's help, or a familiar separate space. Do not introduce confinement at the moment you join an important meeting. If the dog cannot stay comfortable in that arrangement, change the work setup rather than testing a new waiting skill under deadline pressure.

Household movement may be part of the transition too. If standing up starts following or chasing around family members, the guide to managing herding around cats and children covers the separate household arrangements. Do not add children running past as your next call-ending distraction.

When should you progress, repeat, or stop?

Progress only when the current version is comfortably manageable across separate opportunities, including normal variations you have already practiced. Change one component at a time. There is no validated trial count or success percentage for this particular outline.

Use behavior rather than silence alone

Look for an easy return to resting or another ordinary activity, not merely the absence of barking while the dog watches intensely. Compare the same signal at the same difficulty. A quiet practice trial and the end of a long real meeting are different observations, even if both contain the word goodbye.

If your dog is following persistently, repeatedly vocalizing, jumping, or mouthing, stop the rehearsal and reorganize the situation. Do not keep increasing volume or movement to see what happens. A qualified reward-based trainer can observe the sequence and help decide whether your interpretation and starting point fit the individual dog.

Take a useful record to the trainer

Bring your cue sequence, the exact rehearsal version, what your dog did first, and what you did next. Ask which response to reinforce, how to distinguish comfortable disengagement from waiting tensely, and what would make the trainer end a trial. Have them review your plan before combining difficult triggers. Do not describe this article as having received an individual trainer assessment.

For a young dog, also keep expectations practical. The Border Collie teenage-phase guide provides broader training context, but age alone does not tell you why a particular call ending matters.

Common shortcuts that can complicate the ending

The convenient plan is the one you can repeat clearly, not the one with the most cues. Keep the observation, resting skill, and availability invitation distinct. These common setup problems are useful things to check in your log.

  • Practicing the whole chain immediately. Saying goodbye, closing the lid, standing up, and reaching for a ball hides which part the dog noticed.
  • Making every ending mean play. Decide when interaction is genuinely available and use the separate invitation for that moment.
  • Waiting through an escalating reaction. End the rehearsal, simplify it, and seek help if you cannot find a comfortable starting version.
  • Rewarding only after repeated interruptions. Arrange opportunities to recognize quiet disengagement earlier rather than making a disruption the route into every practice sequence.
  • Repeating the availability phrase while still busy. Save it for the interaction you can deliver.
  • Comparing unlike trials. Keep mock endings and real calls separately labeled; do not turn mixed observations into a success rate.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my Border Collie react before I say goodbye?

The relevant signal might be a change in your voice, posture, hand movement, or equipment. Observe the earliest response rather than assuming the final word is the only cue.

Should I stop saying goodbye on work calls?

You can alter your work setup temporarily, but changing one word does not show which signal matters. Practice an easy version separately and keep the actual meeting ending out of demanding experiments.

Does closing the laptop need a separate exercise?

It can, if lid or hand movement is the first thing your dog notices. Begin with a smaller movement without the goodbye, meeting sound, or standing up, and keep the trial comfortable.

What if my dog is quiet but staring at me?

Do not count silence alone as relaxed settling. Look for comfortable disengagement and ordinary activity, and ask a reward-based trainer to help interpret the response if you are unsure.

How many repetitions will fix the problem?

This outline has no validated repetition count or completion deadline. Compare similar, comfortable opportunities and simplify the next setup if the response becomes persistent or intense.

Is this a trainer-verified protocol for my dog?

No. It is an editorial practice outline grounded in general reward-based guidance. Have a qualified reward-based trainer assess your dog and review the plan, especially before combining difficult signals.

A simpler finish starts before the call ends

When your Border Collie gets excited when work calls end, start with a small observation: which sound, word, or movement comes first? Practice an easier version away from meetings, keep a real resting skill separate from waiting intensely, and invite interaction only when you can provide it. A short, repeatable transition and a useful record give you and your trainer something concrete to work with.
