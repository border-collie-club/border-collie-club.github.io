Border Collie Teenage Phase: What Happens From 6 to 24 Months and How to Get Through It

A month-by-month map of Border Collie adolescence, what the research says about "forgotten" training, and a practical plan for the hard weeks.

Home›Blog›Border Collie Teenage Phase

Published: 2026-10-13 · Border Collie Club

The Border Collie teenage phase usually starts somewhere around six months, when the puppy who knew sit, down and a solid recall suddenly behaves as if you are a stranger shouting in a foreign language. If you are reading this at 11 p.m. after a walk that went sideways, wondering whether you are simply not experienced enough for this breed, you are not alone and you are not failing. This guide walks through what changes month by month, what the research actually shows, and a plan you can start tomorrow.

Quick answer: Most Border Collies hit adolescence between 6 and 9 months, are at their most testing somewhere between 8 and 18 months, and settle into young adulthood between roughly 18 and 36 months. Obedience to the owner really does dip during this window (it was measured in a 2020 study), fear reactions can pop up out of nowhere, and the best results come from shorter training sessions, more management, calm handling, and holding off on high-impact sport until the skeleton is mature. The phase passes. Owners who keep the routine steady usually get a better adult dog than the one they had at five months.

When does the Border Collie teenage phase start and end?

For most Border Collies, adolescence begins between 6 and 9 months and the hardest stretch runs to about 18 months, with emotional maturity arriving somewhere between 2 and 3 years. There is no single switch-on date, because puberty timing varies between individual dogs, between sexes, and between working and show lines.

Veterinary behaviorists stress that the stage lasts longer than most owners expect. In an interview with dvm360, board-certified veterinary behaviorist Dr. Christopher Pachel (DACVB) noted that dogs at 14, 16 or 18 months are still "very, very much in that teenager phase," and described adolescence as a period of impulsivity, emotional volatility and hormonally driven behavior. He also suggested a veterinary recheck around 8 months of age, which is a useful checkpoint for a Border Collie too.

Owners report a long tail

Ask experienced Border Collie people and the end date gets pushed back further. In a well-upvoted r/BorderCollie thread on surviving the teenage phase, the top reply put real maturity "somewhere between 2 and 3," and another owner described a dog who was "super hyper and silly for about 2 years" before settling. That is anecdote, not data, but it is a sensible planning horizon: think in terms of two years, not six months.

What actually happens month by month?

The short version: the brain that controls impulses matures more slowly than the body and the hormones, so for a while your dog has adult energy, adult speed and adolescent judgment. The table below is a practical map, not a medical timeline; individual dogs move through it at different speeds.

Why Border Collies can feel harder than other breeds

Border Collies were bred to notice movement instantly and act on it. During adolescence that sensitivity is turned up while self-control is still under construction, so a jogger, a skateboard or a flock of pigeons can trigger a reaction that a less visually driven breed would ignore. Some owners also notice that loud sounds such as trucks or fireworks bother their dog more in this phase. If you already worry about repetitive habits like shadow or light chasing, our overview of Border Collie obsessive behavior statistics explains where normal drive ends and a problem begins.

Does my teenage Border Collie really "forget" their training?

No. The training is still there, but adolescent dogs become measurably less responsive to their own caregiver for a while, which is exactly why it feels personal. It is a stage of development, not proof that you trained badly.

The best evidence comes from a 2020 study led by Dr. Lucy Asher with Newcastle, Nottingham and Edinburgh universities and the charity Guide Dogs. According to the Newcastle University summary of the research, the team tested 69 Labradors, Golden Retrievers and crossbreeds at 5 months and again at 8 months. At 8 months the dogs took longer to respond to "sit" from their caregiver, and were more likely to repeatedly not respond, yet their responses to a stranger actually improved. In a second group of 285 dogs, caregivers gave lower trainability scores around adolescence than at 5 or 12 months, while the less familiar trainers did not.

Border Collies were not in that study, so treat it as a general picture of canine adolescence rather than a breed statistic. Still, the pattern is the one Border Collie owners describe constantly: "He's perfect at class and ignores me at home." That is not evidence that the instructor is a better handler than you. It is evidence that your dog is testing the relationship that matters most to them.

The attachment finding that should reassure you

The same research found that dogs with insecure attachment to their caregivers showed more pronounced adolescent behavior, and that insecurely attached females were more likely to reach puberty early. In other words, a warm, predictable relationship is protective. Asher's own advice was that owners "need to be aware that their dog is going through a phase and it will pass." Staying consistent, and not withdrawing out of frustration, is the single most useful thing you can do.

Is my adolescent Border Collie scared, or just being difficult?

Often both, and fear is easy to mistake for defiance. A teenage Border Collie who suddenly refuses to pass a parked trash can, barks at a neighbor they have met a dozen times, or freezes on a familiar route is usually going through a fear reaction, not making a choice to disobey.

What a teenage fear reaction looks like

  • Freezing, staring hard, or refusing to move forward on a familiar walk
  • Barking or lunging at people, dogs or vehicles that did not bother them a month ago
  • Startling at sounds such as trucks, fireworks or thunder and taking longer to recover
  • Hiding, low body posture, tail tucked, or turning away from greetings

How to respond

Increase distance until your dog can look at the trigger and still take food, then reward calm observation. Do not drag them past it, and do not punish growling or barking, because that only removes the warning signal. One r/BorderCollie member in the thread linked above recommended training first with traffic noise playing at home and only later visiting busier places, which is a sensible gradual approach. If a fear does not fade over a few weeks, or gets worse, involve your veterinarian and a qualified behavior professional rather than waiting it out.

How much exercise does a teenage Border Collie need without hurting their joints?

More mental work and less repetitive pounding is the safest formula until the skeleton is mature. Your adolescent Border Collie may look like an adult, but their growth plates may still be open, and repetitive high-impact exercise is the kind most likely to cause trouble.

In a PetMD article by veterinarian Dr. Vivian Carroll, forced exercise is defined as "anything beyond what the dog would engage in with dogs of the same age." The article lists jogging with an owner and repeated ball or Frisbee chasing as examples, links forced exercise to a higher risk of elbow dysplasia and osteochondrosis, notes that growth plates in large-breed dogs generally close around 12 months, and recommends waiting until 12 to 15 months before more strenuous activity. Border Collies are a medium breed, but individual dogs vary, so the honest answer is to ask your vet. An X-ray can confirm whether growth plates have closed before you start jump training or running together.

Better outlets for the teenage engine

  • Sniff walks on a long line in varied places, at the dog's pace.
  • Short training bursts of a few minutes, ending while the dog is still keen.
  • Foundation sport skills such as body awareness, targeting and low-height work, without repetitive jumping.
  • Calm settle practice on a mat. An overtired adolescent Border Collie often behaves worse, not better.
  • Puzzle feeding and food-scatter games to tire the brain without strain on joints.

Rest is training too. Teaching an off switch now pays off later, including the day you need to leave the dog at home. Our guide on whether Border Collies can be left alone covers realistic time limits and how to build up alone-time safely.

Should I spay or neuter my Border Collie during adolescence?

For Border Collies specifically, the best available data suggests waiting until after 12 months. This is a decision to make with your veterinarian, but the breed-level numbers are worth knowing before that conversation.

A 2020 University of California, Davis analysis of 15 years of hospital records, published in Frontiers in Veterinary Science by Hart and colleagues, included 399 Border Collies. Neutering was not associated with an increased risk of joint disorders in either sex, with 2 to 3 percent of intact dogs diagnosed. Cancers were a different story: one or more of the tracked cancers occurred in 2 percent of intact males and in no intact females, but rose to 13 percent in males neutered at 6 to 11 months and 11 percent in females spayed at 6 to 11 months. Based on that, the authors suggested holding off on neutering both sexes until beyond a year of age.

Hormones also play a part in teenage behavior, but surgery is not a training plan. Marking, roaming and mounting can be handled with management and supervision while you and your vet decide on timing.

A week-by-week survival plan for the teenage months

You do not need a new method. You need a narrower focus and more patience than feels natural. This checklist works for most adolescent Border Collies and can be repeated in two-week blocks.

  1. Drop your criteria, briefly. Go back one step on any skill that has slipped. If recall fails at 30 feet, practice at 10 feet with bigger rewards until it is reliable again.
  2. Manage what you cannot train yet. Long lines, baby gates and leashes are not failures. They stop your dog rehearsing the behavior you are trying to fix, such as chasing cars.
  3. Keep sessions short. Several sessions of a few minutes beat one long session with a dog who has already checked out.
  4. Schedule rest. Build quiet time into every day. Many adolescent Border Collies need help learning to switch off.
  5. Swap intensity for variety. New places, surfaces and sniffing opportunities tire the brain without straining joints.
  6. Book the 8-month vet check. Use it to talk about growth, pain, neuter timing and any fear or reactivity you are seeing.
  7. Track one win a day. Write down one thing that went well. On hard weeks, the list reminds you that progress is real.
  8. Find a positive reinforcement trainer who knows herding breeds, especially if you are dealing with reactivity or fear.

Common mistakes owners make during the teenage phase

Almost everyone makes some of these. Recognizing them early is what separates a rough patch from a lasting problem.

  • Assuming more exercise will fix it. Endless ball throwing tends to build a fitter, more aroused dog and loads growing joints. Mental work and rest usually help more.
  • Taking it personally. The research above shows dogs become less responsive specifically to their caregiver at this age. It is a developmental pattern, not a verdict on you.
  • Punishing fear. Correcting a dog for barking at something scary teaches them that the scary thing predicts trouble.
  • Giving off-leash freedom too early. One successful chase of a car or deer can undo weeks of recall work.
  • Over-socializing. Your teenager does not need to greet every dog. Calm neutrality around other dogs is a better goal.
  • Rehoming in the hardest months. Researchers have pointed out that around 8 months is a vulnerable time, because many dogs are taken to shelters at that age. Get help before deciding the dog is the problem.

Signs you are doing better than you think

Owners of teenage Border Collies often feel like frauds: everyone else's dog seems calm, and theirs is barking at a plastic bag. The fact that you are researching, adjusting and asking questions is exactly what a capable owner does. Look for small signals that the foundation is holding. Your dog checks in with you on walks, even briefly. They recover from a scare faster than last month. They can settle for a few minutes after a training session. They still choose you when it is quiet. Those moments are the adult dog showing up early, and they mean the work is landing.

Frequently asked questions

At what age do Border Collies calm down?

Most Border Collies become noticeably calmer and more focused between 2 and 3 years of age, though many stay high-energy for life. The teenage turbulence itself usually eases after about 18 to 24 months.

Why is my Border Collie good for the trainer but not for me?

Adolescent dogs tend to become less responsive to their own caregiver while responding normally, or even better, to less familiar people. A 2020 study documented exactly this pattern at 8 months. It reflects the stage of development, not poor handling.

Is it normal for a 10-month-old Border Collie to become scared of things?

Yes. New fears of familiar people, objects or sounds are common in adolescence. Give your dog distance, reward calm behavior, avoid forcing exposure, and talk to your vet if the fear lasts more than a few weeks or gets worse.

When can my teenage Border Collie start agility or running with me?

Wait until your vet confirms the growth plates have closed, which may require an X-ray. Veterinary guidance generally suggests holding off on strenuous, repetitive exercise until around 12 to 15 months. Foundation skills without repetitive jumping can start earlier.

Will neutering my Border Collie stop teenage behavior?

Not reliably. Hormones can influence marking and roaming, but training and management do most of the work. For Border Collies, UC Davis data linked neutering at 6 to 11 months with higher cancer rates and suggested waiting until after one year.

How long does the Border Collie teenage phase last?

Expect roughly 6 to 18 months of noticeable adolescent behavior, with full emotional maturity often arriving between 2 and 3 years. For many owners the bumpiest stretch falls somewhere between about 8 and 18 months.

Should I rehome my teenage Border Collie?

Pause before deciding. Adolescence is temporary, and around 8 months is a common age for dogs to be surrendered to shelters. Talk to your vet and a qualified trainer first. Many dogs that seem impossible at 10 months become excellent adults.

The bottom line

The Border Collie teenage phase is real, measurable and temporary. Between about 6 and 24 months your dog will test recall, react to things that never bothered them, and make you question whether you were ready for this breed. You were. Keep sessions short, manage the risky moments, protect growing joints, talk to your vet about neuter timing, and keep showing up with the same calm routine. The adult Border Collie on the other side is worth it. For the next step, keep our Border Collie care checklist handy as your routine settles.
